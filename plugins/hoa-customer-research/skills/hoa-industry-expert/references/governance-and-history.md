# Governance Role and Historical Evolution

## Historical Evolution

The long historical arc is clear: HOA management professionalised as private community governance expanded. Robert Nelson’s Lincoln Institute chapter argues that the rise of community associations since the 1960s represents the emergence of a major new social institution: private community associations acting as decentralised local government in residential settings. His chapter notes that around **1%** of Americans lived in such associations in 1970, versus roughly **20%** by the time of that publication, and explains the shift as part of a broader move towards privately governed housing and neighbourhood services. citeturn17view2

FCAR’s long-run statistical series gives the modern growth trajectory in simple operational terms: from **10,000 associations and 2.1 million residents in 1970** to **222,500 associations and 45.2 million residents in 2000**, then to **373,000 associations and 78.1 million residents in 2025**. FCAR also estimates that **35.2% of U.S. housing** is now in community associations. citeturn42view0

The housing-production pipeline continues to reinforce that role. NAHB’s 2025 analysis of Census Survey of Construction data found that **65.7% of all new single-family homes started in 2024** were built within a community or homeowners association. That means HOA governance is no longer a niche feature attached to selected master-planned developments; it is a mainstream residential operating model for new supply. citeturn33search1turn33search2

Historically, boards often carried far more of the daily administrative burden themselves. FirstService’s own industry description states that day-to-day community operations were historically delegated to volunteer boards, but that boards have increasingly outsourced these responsibilities to professional management companies. That trend makes sense in context: as governance documents, amenities, reserve obligations, insurance complexity, and resident expectations have all grown, the volunteer-board model increasingly requires professional operating support. citeturn14view0turn37view0

A concise timeline helps position that evolution commercially.

| Period | What changed | Why it changed | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1960s–1970s | Community associations emerge as a recognisable private-governance form | Planned communities, condominiums, and private common-area obligations spread | citeturn17view2turn37view0 |
| 1970–2000 | Rapid expansion from 10,000 to 222,500 associations | New residential development increasingly used common-interest governance | citeturn42view0turn17view2 |
| 2000s–2010s | Management becomes more professionalised and specialised | Boards face greater financial, maintenance, and compliance complexity | citeturn14view0turn30view0 |
| 2020s | Greater regulatory scrutiny, insurance volatility, reserve focus, and tech dependence | Asset age, catastrophes, state-law changes, and higher resident expectations increase complexity | citeturn25view0turn20search0turn26view0turn18view0 |

The essential commercial point is that HOA management agencies exist because HOAs fuse **property, finance, governance, and resident relations** into a single operating system. That is unusual in real estate, and it is why adjacent vendors can create real value if they reduce cross-functional friction rather than treating the market as ordinary property management. citeturn37view0turn30view0
