# HOA Industry Overview

> This file contains the broad industry framing, scale, and essential commercial context.

## Executive summary

The HOA management industry is a large, resilient, and still-fragmented operating layer that sits between volunteer boards, homeowners, vendors, and specialist advisers. In the U.S. alone, the Foundation for Community Association Research estimates **373,000 community associations in 2025**, covering **29.6 million housing units** and **78.1 million residents**; it also estimates **9,000–10,000 management companies**, **60,000–65,000 community association managers**, and **30–40% of associations as self-managed**. That combination means the market is both substantial and operationally uneven, with many communities still relying on manual processes or partial outsourcing. citeturn42view0

What HOA management agencies actually do is broader than many boards realise. Core responsibilities typically include budgeting, billing and collections, accounts payable/receivable, financial reporting, maintenance coordination, vendor sourcing and oversight, meeting administration, election support, record-keeping, compliance and rule administration, homeowner communications, reserve-planning coordination, insurance coordination, and after-hours emergency response. State licensing definitions, federal labour-market descriptions, and public service descriptions from large management firms all converge on that picture, though the exact mix depends heavily on the contract. citeturn22view0turn30view0turn34view3turn43view1turn43view3

The industry’s central commercial tension is that boards want professionalisation, but they are intensely price-sensitive and governance-sensitive. Publicly available industry research shows management companies planning growth, but also reporting labour shortages, cost inflation, fee pressure, and a strong need for operational efficiency. In the 2024 industry survey published through CAI resources, **73% of communities reported being professionally managed**, **84% of management companies had expanded portfolios over the prior two years**, **70% planned to raise management fees**, and **55% planned to lean on technology to improve efficiency**. citeturn16view0

The hardest recurring problems for managers and boards are not conceptual; they are coordination failures. Insurance shocks, reserve underfunding, maintenance backlogs, vendor follow-through, records retention, homeowner communications, collections, and state-law compliance all require information to move accurately across multiple parties. Official and quasi-official sources show why these frictions are persistent: insurance premiums have risen sharply in some markets; reserve laws are increasingly prescriptive; boards remain volunteer-led; and managers must often operate across several communities while maintaining responsiveness. citeturn26view0turn20search0turn25view0turn30view0

For sales positioning, the most persuasive angle is usually **not** “replace the manager”. It is “reduce the cost and risk of the manager-board-vendor hand-off”. Boards buy when a solution improves transparency, accelerates routine work, reduces resident friction, protects compliance, and makes transitions easier. Solutions that help with document control, vendor procurement and performance, reserve and capital planning workflows, resident self-service, compliance calendars, collections visibility, and emergency communications map directly to the industry’s most persistent operational pain points and procurement criteria. citeturn18view0turn18view1turn38view0turn38view1turn8view1

## Scope and framing

Unless otherwise stated, this report is **U.S.-centred**. That is because the term “HOA” is predominantly U.S.-specific, most authoritative statistics and regulatory texts are U.S.-based, and the clearest management-industry evidence comes from U.S. trade bodies, regulators, and company disclosures. In the official research base, “community association” typically includes **homeowners associations, condominiums, and cooperatives**; FCAR notes that HOAs account for roughly **58–63%** of the total, condominiums **35–40%**, and cooperatives **2–4%**. citeturn37view0turn42view0

HOAs sit inside a wider system of **private residential governance**. Cornell’s Legal Information Institute describes HOAs as the governing bodies of many common-interest communities, with owners bound by CC&Rs and required to pay fees for common-area management and other obligations. In other words, HOA management is not just property administration; it is the day-to-day operating machinery of a private governance structure. citeturn37view0

That governance footprint is economically material. FCAR estimates that homes in community associations were worth about **$13.1 trillion** in 2025, that associations collected about **$124.2 billion** in assessments, and that reserve-fund contributions totalled about **$31.1 billion**. The U.S. Census Bureau separately reported that **21.6 million owner households** paid condo or HOA fees in 2024, with a **national median monthly fee of $135**, while fee burdens varied sharply by state. citeturn42view0turn41view0
