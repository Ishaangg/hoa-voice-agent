# Industry Structure and Business Models

## Operating Models

The industry is structured around a spectrum running from **self-management** to **directly employed on-site managers** to **third-party management firms**. CAI’s selection guide frames the main choices explicitly: a board may self-manage, hire an on-site manager, or contract with a community association management company. FCAR estimates that **30–40%** of associations remain self-managed, although a 2024 industry survey found **73%** of communities reporting professional management; the studies use different methods, but both support the same conclusion: professional management is widespread, while a sizeable minority still self-manage or use partial outsourcing. citeturn8view1turn42view0turn16view0

At the outsourced end of the spectrum, a particularly useful distinction comes from FirstService’s annual information form, which separates **traditional management** from **full-service management**. In its description, traditional managers focus mainly on administrative and governance functions, including advice to boards, collection of fees, supplier payment, financial statements, and outsourcing of support services. Full-service managers do all of that **plus** on-site staffing, engineering and maintenance, amenity management, security or concierge, and ancillary products such as banking, insurance, energy management, and resale processing. FirstService also states that these contracts are generally written as **fixed monthly fee** arrangements, typically **one to three years** in duration, though often cancellable on **30 to 90 days’ notice**. citeturn14view0

Scale matters because service models change materially by community complexity.

“Portfolio management” is also a common operating pattern in the market, especially where a single manager supports multiple communities rather than being dedicated on site. However, authoritative national data specifying the share of portfolio versus on-site arrangements are **not clearly published** in the source set reviewed here. What is clear is that portfolio-style job titles and support structures are common throughout the industry, while large-scale communities skew on-site. citeturn32search10turn31search14

Regional differences are most clearly visible **within the U.S.**, and they are mainly driven by **state law, housing form, insurance conditions, and development patterns**. California requires reserve studies with a reasonably competent visual inspection at least every three years and annual review of reserve funding assumptions. Florida licenses both CAMs and CAM firms above certain size/budget thresholds, specifies continuing-education areas including legal update, insurance, financial management, physical property, and record-keeping, and treats community association management as a regulated profession. Nevada also certifies community managers. Meanwhile, FCAR’s state estimates show the biggest concentration of associations in states such as **California, Florida, Texas, Illinois, and North Carolina**, while Census data show higher HOA/condo fee prevalence in places such as **Arizona, Florida, and Nevada**, and very high fee burdens in **New York, the District of Columbia, and Hawaii**. Outside the U.S., authoritative region-by-region HOA-management differences are **not well specified** in the sources reviewed. citeturn22view0turn22view1turn22view3turn23search12turn42view0turn41view0
